## The Continuing History Of WBS.

_WBS INFO · Jan 30, 2011|44_

> CHAPTER 22
>
> The consoles with the dot matrices are a bit of a sad story for me. The console shown in the WBS ad (WBS ads were always color outside back cover) was one of three built for Westinghouse Broadcasting in San Francisco, which I believe was channel 5 at that time. The chief engineer was Merrill Weiss, and he wanted some very special design items in his consoles (2 on-air studio control rooms and 1 production control room).
>
> As one example of custom design for Merrill, all of the power supplies were arranged in pairs in a redundant fashion. In each pair PS 1 and PS 2 would feed a load not exceeding 1/2 of its capacity. If a supply were to fail, its load would be switched to the good supply. This function was monitored and operated by a control/switch combination consisting of 2 green indicators (PS 1 & PS 2) over a toggle switch (PS 1/NORMAL/PS2) over a RED indicator. If a supply failed (GN indicator dim or OFF), you pushed the toggle toward the good indicator. The RED indicator below told you that the toggle was not in the normal position so that with two good supplies you didn't drive both loads from just one. This idea was originally engineered for the CBS consoles, but was used often through the 1970's and 1980's. The control clusters can be seen in the ad at the end of the console by the TB mic, and on the rack above those PS units.
>
> The dot matrix readouts above the fader read the LINE level source assigned to that channel. These line level sources came from a ROUTING SWITCHER designed by Rodger for these consoles. WBS had built audio switchers from time to time (see M804, M808, M812 and similar modules) but this was the first and only time Rodger designed a control system that read out in the write-on strip in the console in four digit alpha numeric characters which could be pre-programmed to for each switcher source (VT02, REM5, etc.). The sad thing for me was that WBS never sold this sophisticated control and indicator system to any other customer once it had been "invented".
